# Inventory
## Purpose
Inventory defines how SAB’A manages, controls, monitors, and plans the materials and products held throughout the operational system.
The objective is to maintain sufficient availability while controlling excess stock, waste, storage requirements, and working capital.
## Inventory Principles
1. **Accuracy before Assumption**
2. **Availability without Excess**
3. **Visibility before Action**
4. **Control without Unnecessary Complexity**
5. **Traceability where Required**
6. **Planning before Urgency**
7. **Quality throughout Storage**
8. **Inventory Decisions based on Reliable Information**
## Inventory Categories
SAB’A inventory may include:
- Raw Materials
- Fragrance Materials
- Packaging Components
- Work in Progress
- Finished Products
- Samples
- Promotional Materials
- Operational Supplies
- Damaged or Quarantined Stock
- Returned Products
Each category should be managed according to its characteristics and requirements.
## Inventory Structure
Inventory should provide visibility into:
- Item
- SKU or reference
- Quantity
- Unit
- Location
- Status
- Batch or lot where applicable
- Date received
- Expiry or recommended use date where applicable
- Reserved quantity
- Available quantity
Inventory records should remain sufficiently accurate to support operational and commercial decisions.
## Stock Status
Where appropriate, inventory should distinguish between:
- **Available** — Ready for approved use or sale.
- **Reserved** — Allocated to a specific requirement or customer order.
- **Quarantined** — Temporarily restricted pending review.
- **Damaged** — Not suitable for normal use or sale.
- **Returned** — Received back from a customer and awaiting assessment.
- **Expired** — No longer approved for intended use.
Stock status should be clear enough to prevent unintended use or sale.
## Inventory Planning
Inventory planning should consider:
- Demand
- Sales history
- Forecasts
- Supplier lead times
- Production lead times
- Minimum order quantities
- Launch requirements
- Seasonal demand
- Safety stock
- Storage capacity
- Cash availability
Planning should aim to reduce both stockouts and unnecessary inventory accumulation.
## Stock Levels
Where practical, SAB’A should establish appropriate inventory thresholds.
These may include:
- Minimum stock level
- Reorder point
- Target stock level
- Safety stock
- Maximum stock level
Thresholds should reflect actual demand, lead times, risk, and business requirements.
## Receiving
Incoming inventory should be checked against the relevant purchase or delivery documentation.
Receiving checks may include:
- Quantity
- Item identity
- Condition
- Packaging integrity
- Batch or lot information
- Required documentation
- Visible damage
- Specification where applicable
Discrepancies should be documented and resolved appropriately.
## Storage
Inventory should be stored according to the requirements of the specific material or product.
Storage considerations may include:
- Temperature
- Humidity
- Light exposure
- Cleanliness
- Security
- Physical protection
- Separation of incompatible materials
- Access control
Storage conditions should protect product quality and operational safety.
## Inventory Movement
Inventory movements should be recorded where necessary.
Examples include:
- Receiving
- Production consumption
- Transfers
- Adjustments
- Reservations
- Dispatch
- Returns
- Disposal
Material movements should remain traceable where traceability is required.
## Batch and Lot Control
Materials and products requiring batch or lot control should retain appropriate identification throughout their operational lifecycle.
Batch information may support:
- Traceability
- Quality investigation
- Recall management
- Supplier review
- Production records
- Customer issue investigation
## Stock Counting
Inventory should be periodically verified through appropriate counting procedures.
Counting frequency may depend on:
- Inventory value
- Operational importance
- Movement frequency
- Risk
- Historical discrepancies
Differences between physical and recorded inventory should be investigated rather than simply corrected without explanation.
## Stock Adjustments
Inventory adjustments should be:
- Justified
- Documented
- Reviewed where appropriate
- Traceable to the underlying issue
Repeated adjustments may indicate a process problem and should trigger investigation.
## Slow-Moving and Excess Inventory
Slow-moving or excess inventory should be identified periodically.
Potential actions may include:
- Demand review
- Production adjustment
- Purchasing adjustment
- Reallocation
- Product bundling
- Controlled promotional activity
- Alternative approved use
- Disposal where necessary
Actions should protect product quality and brand value.
## Stockouts
Stockouts should be monitored and investigated when they materially affect customers or operations.
Root causes may include:
- Forecasting errors
- Supplier delays
- Production constraints
- Unexpected demand
- Inventory inaccuracies
- Fulfillment issues
Corrective action should address the cause rather than repeatedly treating the symptom.
## Damaged, Returned, and Quarantined Stock
Restricted inventory should be clearly separated from available inventory.
Assessment should determine whether the item can be:
- Returned to available stock
- Reworked
- Repackaged
- Returned to supplier
- Used for an approved alternative purpose
- Disposed of
No restricted inventory should be released without appropriate approval.
## Inventory and Fulfillment
Inventory information should remain synchronized with ecommerce and fulfillment systems where applicable.
Customer-facing availability should reflect realistic stock levels to reduce:
- Overselling
- Cancellations
- Delays
- Customer dissatisfaction
## Inventory Performance
Inventory performance may be monitored through:
- Stock accuracy
- Stock availability
- Stockout frequency
- Inventory turnover
- Days of inventory
- Slow-moving inventory
- Excess inventory
- Inventory value
- Waste
- Order fulfillment impact
Metrics should support better inventory decisions rather than create unnecessary administrative work.
## Risk Management
Inventory risks may include:
- Stockouts
- Overstock
- Damage
- Expiry
- Storage failure
- Inventory inaccuracies
- Theft or loss
- Supplier disruption
- Demand volatility
Critical risks should have appropriate controls or contingency plans.
## Documentation and Records
Inventory records may include:
- Stock records
- Receiving records
- Batch records
- Inventory counts
- Adjustments
- Transfers
- Disposal records
- Quarantine records
- Return records
Records should be maintained according to operational, financial, quality, and regulatory requirements.
## Core Principle
> Inventory should give SAB’A confidence that what is recorded, what is available, and what can be delivered are aligned.
