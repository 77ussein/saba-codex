# Operations Architecture
## Purpose
Operations Architecture defines the structure through which SAB’A plans, coordinates, executes, and monitors its operational activities.
It provides a clear relationship between suppliers, materials, production, quality, inventory, fulfillment, and supporting functions.
The architecture should make operations understandable, controlled, and scalable.
## Operational Structure
SAB’A operations are organized around the following core flow:
**Planning**  
→ **Procurement**  
→ **Supplier Management**  
→ **Production**  
→ **Quality Control**  
→ **Inventory**  
→ **Fulfillment**  
→ **Customer**
Supporting functions operate across the entire system:
- Documentation
- Forecasting
- Cost Control
- Risk Management
- Performance Monitoring
- Compliance
## Planning
Planning translates business and product requirements into operational requirements.
Planning may include:
- Demand forecasting
- Production requirements
- Procurement requirements
- Inventory targets
- Capacity planning
- Launch requirements
- Fulfillment requirements
Operational planning should reflect current business priorities and approved product plans.
## Procurement
Procurement ensures that required materials, components, and services are available when needed.
It should coordinate:
- Requirements
- Quantities
- Specifications
- Suppliers
- Lead times
- Costs
- Purchase schedules
Procurement decisions should follow approved product and quality specifications.
## Supplier Management
Suppliers are an integral part of the operational system.
Supplier management should address:
- Supplier selection
- Qualification
- Documentation
- Performance
- Communication
- Lead times
- Quality
- Capacity
- Risk
Critical suppliers should have appropriate monitoring and contingency planning.
## Production
Production converts approved materials and specifications into finished products.
Production should operate according to:
- Approved specifications
- Quality standards
- Production procedures
- Batch requirements
- Safety requirements
- Documentation requirements
Production records should provide sufficient traceability for critical activities.
## Quality Control
Quality control provides verification throughout the operational process.
Quality checks may occur at:
- Incoming materials
- Production stages
- Finished products
- Packaging
- Fulfillment
Quality requirements should be defined before production rather than determined after problems occur.
## Inventory
Inventory connects procurement, production, and fulfillment.
Inventory management should provide visibility into:
- Raw materials
- Components
- Packaging
- Work in progress
- Finished products
- Available stock
- Reserved stock
- Damaged or quarantined stock
Inventory information should remain accurate enough to support operational and commercial decisions.
## Fulfillment
Fulfillment manages the movement of confirmed customer orders through preparation, dispatch, and delivery.
The process should include:
**Order Confirmation**  
→ **Order Processing**  
→ **Picking**  
→ **Packing**  
→ **Dispatch**  
→ **Tracking**  
→ **Delivery**
Exceptions should be identified and managed through defined procedures.
## Information Flow
Operational information should move between relevant functions without unnecessary duplication.
Important information may include:
- Product specifications
- Purchase requirements
- Supplier information
- Inventory levels
- Production status
- Quality results
- Order information
- Shipping status
- Operational issues
The current approved documentation should remain the primary reference for operational decisions.
## Decision Flow
Operational decisions should follow an appropriate hierarchy:
**Approved Standard**  
→ **Operational Requirement**  
→ **Execution**  
→ **Verification**  
→ **Record**  
→ **Review**
When an established standard does not adequately address a situation, the issue should be documented and resolved through the appropriate decision process.
## Traceability
Critical operational activities should be traceable where practical.
Traceability may include:
- Supplier
- Material
- Batch
- Production date
- Quality result
- Inventory movement
- Order
- Fulfillment record
The required level of traceability should reflect the operational and regulatory importance of the activity.
## Exception Management
Operational exceptions may include:
- Supplier delays
- Material shortages
- Production deviations
- Quality failures
- Inventory discrepancies
- Order errors
- Fulfillment delays
Exceptions should be:
1. Identified
2. Documented
3. Assessed
4. Resolved
5. Reviewed when necessary
Repeated exceptions should trigger investigation rather than repeated temporary fixes.
## Risk and Continuity
The operational architecture should account for risks that could interrupt supply, production, or fulfillment.
Critical dependencies should be identified and monitored.
Where appropriate, SAB’A should maintain:
- Alternative suppliers
- Safety stock
- Backup processes
- Contingency procedures
- Recovery plans
## Performance Monitoring
The architecture should support monitoring of:
- Supplier performance
- Procurement lead times
- Production performance
- Quality performance
- Inventory availability
- Fulfillment accuracy
- Delivery performance
- Operational cost
Performance data should be used to improve the system continuously.
## Scalability
The operational architecture should support increasing:
- Product range
- Order volume
- Supplier count
- Production volume
- Geographic reach
- Customer demand
Growth should increase structure and control where necessary without creating unnecessary bureaucracy.
## Core Principle
> SAB’A operations should function as one connected system, where information, materials, quality, and customer experience move together with clarity and control.
