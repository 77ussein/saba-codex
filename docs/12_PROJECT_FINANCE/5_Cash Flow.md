# Cash Flow

## Purpose

The Cash Flow document defines how SAB’A monitors, plans, and manages the movement of cash into and out of the business.

The objective is to protect liquidity, maintain operational continuity, anticipate financial pressure, and ensure that SAB’A can meet its obligations while pursuing sustainable growth.

## Cash Flow Principles

1. **Liquidity before Appearance**
2. **Cash Visibility**
3. **Forward Planning**
4. **Commitment Awareness**
5. **Timing Matters**
6. **Conservative Assumptions**
7. **Maintain Financial Resilience**
8. **Protect Operational Continuity**

## Cash Flow Philosophy

Profitability and cash flow are related but not identical.

A business may report accounting profit while experiencing cash pressure due to:

- Inventory Purchases
- Supplier Deposits
- Delayed Customer Payments
- Capital Expenditure
- Taxes
- Large Production Commitments
- Other Timing Differences

SAB’A should therefore monitor cash independently from reported profitability.

## Cash Flow Structure

Cash flow may be understood through three broad categories:

### Operating Cash Flow

Cash generated or consumed through normal business operations.

Examples include:

- Customer Payments
- Supplier Payments
- Manufacturing
- Shipping
- Marketing
- Payroll
- Software
- Professional Services
- Taxes and Duties where applicable

### Investing Cash Flow

Cash associated with longer-term investments.

Examples include:

- Equipment
- Technology Infrastructure
- Product Development Assets
- Other Long-Term Investments

### Financing Cash Flow

Cash associated with funding the business.

Examples may include:

- Owner Contributions
- Financing
- Loans
- Capital Transactions
- Debt Repayments
- Distributions where applicable

The appropriate accounting classification should follow the applicable accounting framework.

## Cash Position

SAB’A should maintain visibility over:

- Opening Cash
- Expected Cash Inflows
- Expected Cash Outflows
- Committed Payments
- Available Cash
- Closing Cash

A basic planning model may be represented as:

> Opening Cash + Cash Inflows − Cash Outflows = Closing Cash

## Cash Inflows

Potential cash inflows may include:

- Ecommerce Sales
- Retail Sales
- Wholesale Payments
- Corporate Sales
- Partnerships
- Owner Contributions
- Financing
- Other Approved Sources

Expected inflows should be based on realistic assumptions rather than optimistic forecasts.

## Cash Outflows

Potential cash outflows may include:

- Raw Materials
- Packaging
- Manufacturing
- Inventory
- Shipping
- Fulfillment
- Marketing
- Salaries
- Software
- Professional Services
- Rent where applicable
- Taxes
- Duties
- Capital Expenditure
- Debt Payments

Cash planning should consider both recurring and one-time obligations.

## Cash Flow Forecast

SAB’A should maintain a forward-looking cash flow forecast appropriate to the stage and complexity of the business.

The forecast may include:

| Period | Opening Cash | Inflows | Outflows | Net Cash Flow | Closing Cash |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Period 1 | — | — | — | — | — |
| Period 2 | — | — | — | — | — |
| Period 3 | — | — | — | — | — |
| Period 4 | — | — | — | — | — |

The forecast should be updated when material assumptions change.

## Forecast Assumptions

Cash flow forecasts should clearly identify assumptions relating to:

- Sales
- Collection Timing
- Production
- Supplier Terms
- Inventory Purchases
- Marketing Spend
- Operating Expenses
- Taxes
- Capital Expenditure
- Financing

Uncertain assumptions should be clearly distinguished from confirmed commitments.

## Committed Cash

Cash planning should distinguish between:

- Actual Payments
- Approved Payments
- Contractual Commitments
- Expected Payments
- Optional Spending

A significant commitment may affect future liquidity even before the payment is made.

## Working Capital

Cash flow should be closely connected to working capital management.

Key areas include:

- Inventory
- Supplier Payment Terms
- Customer Receivables
- Accounts Payable
- Operating Cash

Excess inventory can tie up cash and reduce financial flexibility.

## Inventory and Cash

Inventory purchases should be evaluated not only by unit cost but also by their effect on available cash.

Before significant inventory commitments, SAB’A should consider:

- Expected Demand
- Production Lead Time
- Minimum Order Quantity
- Storage Requirements
- Sales Velocity
- Cash Requirement
- Risk of Slow-Moving Inventory

A lower unit cost does not automatically justify a larger inventory commitment.

## Supplier Payment Terms

Supplier payment terms can materially affect cash flow.

Relevant terms may include:

- Deposit Requirements
- Payment on Order
- Payment Before Shipment
- Net Payment Terms
- Milestone Payments

Payment commitments should be incorporated into cash flow planning.

## Customer Collections

Where customers or commercial partners pay after delivery, expected collection timing should be reflected in the forecast.

Relevant considerations may include:

- Payment Terms
- Expected Collection Date
- Outstanding Receivables
- Delayed Payments
- Credit Risk

Revenue should not automatically be treated as immediately available cash.

## Cash Reserve

Where financially appropriate, SAB’A should maintain a cash reserve to provide resilience against:

- Unexpected Costs
- Revenue Volatility
- Supplier Changes
- Production Delays
- Emergency Requirements
- Market Conditions

The appropriate reserve level should reflect the scale, risk, and operating model of the business.

## Cash Flow Scenarios

Where uncertainty is significant, SAB’A may model multiple cash flow scenarios.

Examples:

- Conservative
- Base
- Growth

Scenario planning should identify periods where cash may become constrained.

## Cash Flow Pressure

Potential warning signs include:

- Rapid Cash Reduction
- Increasing Inventory
- Delayed Customer Collections
- Rising Supplier Commitments
- Persistent Negative Operating Cash Flow
- Large Upcoming Payments
- Declining Gross Margin
- Dependence on Unexpected Financing

Material warning signs should trigger review and appropriate action.

## Cash Flow Controls

Appropriate controls may include:

- Payment Approval
- Bank Reconciliation
- Cash Forecast Review
- Expense Monitoring
- Supplier Payment Scheduling
- Receivables Monitoring
- Purchase Authorization
- Commitment Tracking

Controls should remain proportionate to the size and complexity of SAB’A.

## Cash Flow and Growth

Growth can increase cash requirements.

Higher sales may require:

- More Inventory
- Larger Production Runs
- More Packaging
- Increased Fulfillment
- Higher Marketing Spend
- Larger Working Capital Requirements

SAB’A should therefore assess the cash implications of growth before scaling commitments.

## Cash Flow and Investment

Significant investments should be evaluated against available cash and expected future cash requirements.

Before committing capital, SAB’A should consider:

- Immediate Cash Impact
- Expected Return
- Payback Period
- Operating Requirements
- Alternative Uses of Cash
- Downside Risk

## Cash Flow Review

Cash flow should be reviewed at an appropriate frequency based on business activity and financial risk.

The review may consider:

- Actual Cash Position
- Forecast Cash Position
- Material Variances
- Upcoming Commitments
- Expected Receipts
- Inventory Purchases
- Major Expenses
- Financing Requirements

## Cash Flow Records

Cash flow records should preserve sufficient information to understand:

- Actual Inflows
- Actual Outflows
- Forecasts
- Forecast Changes
- Material Variances
- Major Commitments
- Cash Position Over Time

Historical records should be retained where useful for financial analysis and planning.

## Relationship with Other Financial Documents

Cash Flow should work together with:

- Financial Framework
- Budget
- Costing
- Pricing Model
- Financial Records

The Budget explains planned allocation.

Costing explains product economics.

Pricing determines commercial value.

Cash Flow explains when money actually enters and leaves the business.

## Professional Financial Advice

Accounting, taxation, financing, treasury, and other specialized financial matters should be reviewed with appropriately qualified professionals where required.

This document establishes an internal cash flow management framework and does not replace professional financial or accounting advice.

## Core Principle

> SAB’A should never confuse money earned on paper with money available to operate the business.
